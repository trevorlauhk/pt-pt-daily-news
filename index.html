This morning, Sofia woke up early. She had an idea: she wanted to send a letter to her grandfather. Her grandfather lives in the Algarve, and Sofia misses him a lot. After breakfast, Sofia went to her room and wrote a beautiful letter. She talked about how school is going, mentioned her friends, and even drew a sun on the page. "Mom, I'm going to the post office!" Sofia said. "Great! But take some money because you need to buy a stamp," her mother replied. Sofia took her wallet and left the house. The post office is near the park, so she walked there. On the way, she saw a friend from school. When she arrived at the post office, Sofia waited in line. "Good morning! I'd like to send this letter, please," Sofia said to the lady at the counter. Sofia paid and stuck the stamp on the envelope. Now, Sofia will wait for her grandfather's reply.

English

In recent years, Lisbon and Porto have been the stage for a housing crisis that seems to have no end. Despite the government implementing several measures, such as increasing the supply of social housing, many citizens continue to struggle with exorbitant rents and a lack of affordable options. It is undeniable that the situation has worsened with the tourism boom and real estate speculation, which have turned historic neighborhoods into veritable theme parks for visitors. However, we must not forget that behind the numbers and statistics, there are entire families living in precarious conditions, dreaming of a space they can call home. One of the main reasons for this problem is that wages in Portugal have not kept pace with housing prices. While a young professional might earn, on average, 800 euros per month, a one-bedroom apartment in central Lisbon easily costs over 1000 euros. Therefore, it is understandable that many choose to share housing or, in extreme cases, leave the city. However, this is not a sustainable solution, as it perpetuates a cycle of instability and uncertainty. Some argue that the solution lies in limiting short-term rentals, so that more properties can be made available to residents. Others believe it is necessary to invest in fairer rental policies, ensuring that no one is evicted without viable alternatives. Either way, it is crucial that authorities act urgently before the crisis becomes irreversible. After all, a city without residents is like a body without a soul—it loses its identity and purpose. Despite everything, there is still hope. Community initiatives, such as housing cooperatives, have emerged to address this need. It is possible that, with effort and solidarity, a balance between economic development and the right to housing can be found. Until then, we must fight for a future where everyone has a roof over their heads.
