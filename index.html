Yesterday, I went to the City Hall with my mom. We needed to take care of some important documents. We left home early because the office opens at nine. When we arrived, there were many people in line. A kind lady told us, "You can take a number over there." So, we took a number and sat down. While we waited, my mom read a newspaper, and I looked at my phone. After twenty minutes, they called our number. We went to the counter, and a clerk helped us. She was very patient and explained everything calmly. In the end, we signed the papers and received a receipt. Before leaving, we stopped by the City Hall café. We had a coffee and ate a cake. My mom said, "Let’s go home. Then, we’ll have lunch out!" It was a tiring day, but everything went well.

English

In recent years, social media has become an unavoidable part of young people's lives. While it offers unique opportunities for connection and learning, many experts wonder whether these digital spaces are actually shaping behaviors in worrying ways. Since teenagers spend hours scrolling through idealized profiles, it's natural that they begin to compare their lives with edited versions of reality. Consequently, issues like low self-esteem and anxiety arise—phenomena that, however, aren't always visible at first glance. Yet, it would be unfair to blame only the platforms. Young people, as long as they have critical guidance, can use social media to their advantage so that they develop essential digital skills. The real challenge lies in balancing exposure with the awareness that not everything that shines on social media is gold. After all, who hasn't felt tempted to share only perfect moments while hiding flaws? Although some studies suggest that social media can worsen feelings of loneliness, others highlight its role in creating support communities. Therefore, the solution isn't to ban it but to educate. If young people learn to filter content and value authenticity, perhaps social media will stop being a prison and finally become a truer mirror of real life.
