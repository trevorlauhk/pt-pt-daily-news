English

A few years ago, when we decided to move to Lisbon, we believed that finding a decent apartment would just be a matter of time. However, reality turned out to be very different. Despite having a reasonable budget, the options were scarce, the prices exorbitant, and often the ads seemed more like a game of chance than a serious search. In Porto, the situation is no less worrying. Entire families are forced to share tiny homes, while young people, even with stable jobs, cannot take the step toward independence. It’s as if the cities had built invisible walls around the dreams of those who want to live in them. Since demand far exceeds supply, prices soar, making housing unaffordable for much of the population. Consequently, many end up giving up on living in central areas, moving to the suburbs, where, although costs are lower, living conditions are not always ideal. However, this is not just a matter of numbers. It is a crisis that affects the social fabric of cities, as it pushes out those who precisely give life to historic neighborhoods. It is urgent that the government implement effective measures so that housing ceases to be a luxury. As long as there is political will, solutions such as increasing the supply of social housing or tax incentives for affordable rent could ease the pressure. After all, a city that does not welcome its inhabitants is like a house without doors: beautiful on the outside, but empty inside.
