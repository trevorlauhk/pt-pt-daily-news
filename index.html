English

Yesterday was a very special day for João. He woke up early, had breakfast, and then went to the beach with his family. The sun was strong, but the wind was also blowing and cooled things down a bit. When they arrived, João ran to the sand and took off his shoes. The water was cold, but he went in slowly. His sister, Maria, stayed on the towel reading a book. His dad swam a little, and his mum took lots of photos. Afterwards, they had lunch at a beachside restaurant. They ate grilled fish, salad, and chips. João loved it! In the afternoon, they played with a ball and built a huge sandcastle. Before leaving, João collected shells to take home. At night, he was tired but happy. He will remember this day forever.

Portugal a Brilhar: Será o Sol a Chave para um Futuro Sustentável?

English

In recent years, Portugal has stood out as one of Europe’s most committed countries to the energy transition. Despite facing significant challenges, such as historical dependence on fossil fuels, the potential of renewable energy in our territory is undeniable. The sun, which bathes the Alentejo for over 300 days a year, and the wind, which blows strongly along the northern coast, are resources that, if well harnessed, could transform the country into a green powerhouse. However, for this to happen, it is essential that the government and companies invest not only in infrastructure but also in innovation and environmental education. One of Portugal’s greatest advantages is its geographical diversity, which allows for the exploration of different clean energy sources. For example, hydropower already accounts for a significant portion of our electricity production, especially in the north, where rivers are abundant. Nevertheless, with climate change threatening river flows, it is crucial that we further diversify our sources. Solar and wind energy, therefore, emerge as viable alternatives, since they do not depend as much on hydrological conditions. Despite the progress made, there are still obstacles to overcome. The resistance of some local communities to the installation of wind or solar farms is a real issue, as many fear these projects may affect the landscape or traditional economies. To address this, transparent dialogue between all involved parties is necessary so that solutions benefiting both the environment and the population can be found. Additionally, slow bureaucracy can delay important projects; thus, simplifying administrative processes would be a key step in accelerating the transition. In conclusion, Portugal has all the conditions to become a European leader in renewable energy. As long as there is political will, continuous investment, and an informed and participative society, the future could be not only sustainable but also prosperous. After all, as the saying goes, "We do not inherit the Earth from our ancestors; we borrow it from our children." It is up to us, therefore, to ensure that this legacy is clean and full of light.
