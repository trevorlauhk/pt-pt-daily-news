Last night, Maria’s shower made a strange noise. At first, she thought, "It must be the wind." But then, the water started to come out very slowly. Maria tried to open the tap more, but it didn’t help. So, she called her husband and said, "Honey, the shower is having problems." Tomorrow we’ll call a plumber. This morning, Maria looked online and found the number of a plumber near her house. She called and said, "Good morning! I need a plumber, please." The man answered, "I can come by at three in the afternoon. Is that okay?" At exactly three o’clock, the plumber arrived. In ten minutes, he replaced a part, and the water started flowing normally again. Maria was very happy and paid him fifty euros. Before leaving, the plumber said, "If you have more problems, call me again." Maria replied, "Thank you very much! Now I’m going to take a hot shower and relax."

English

In recent years, Lisbon and Porto have been the stage for a housing crisis that shows no signs of ending. Although the government has implemented some measures, such as increasing public housing, the truth is that prices continue to rise at an alarming rate. Many young people, even those with stable jobs, are forced to share housing or live on the outskirts, since salaries do not keep up with real estate inflation. However, the problem is not just about prices. Real estate speculation, fueled by tourism and foreign investment, has turned historic neighborhoods into unaffordable zones for locals. It is urgent to find solutions that balance economic development with citizens' quality of life, so that cities do not lose their cultural identity. Nevertheless, not all is lost. Some local initiatives, such as housing cooperatives or urban rehabilitation projects, have shown that alternatives are possible. Therefore, it is crucial that civil society and authorities work together, provided there is transparency and political will. After all, a city is not just made of buildings, but of the people who live in it and the dreams they build. If we do not act now, we risk turning Lisbon and Porto into open-air museums, where only tourists can afford to visit, while the Portuguese are pushed to the outskirts.
