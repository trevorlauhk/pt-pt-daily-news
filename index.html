Rita woke up early. The alarm clock rang at five in the morning, but she didn’t stay in bed. Today is the day! She’s traveling to Lisbon for the first time. After having breakfast, Rita picked up her suitcase and called a taxi. At the airport, she looked at the screen: the flight to Lisbon was leaving at nine. — Good morning! I have my reservation here. The agent smiled and asked for her ticket and passport. The agent weighed the suitcase and gave Rita her boarding pass. Rita thanked her and went to the security area. She took off her shoes and belt and put her backpack in the tray. Then, she walked through the metal detector. Now, Rita is waiting at the boarding gate. She’s a little nervous, but also very happy. Soon, she’ll be flying!

English

In recent years, Portugal has witnessed a significant increase in immigration—a phenomenon that, while bringing numerous economic and cultural benefits, also presents complex challenges to society. It is undeniable that immigrants contribute to the country’s growth, filling labor market gaps and enriching cultural diversity. However, full integration does not happen overnight, and this is where the biggest obstacle lies. Many immigrants arrive with the hope of a better life but face linguistic, bureaucratic, and sometimes prejudicial barriers. It is essential that the State creates effective public policies so that everyone feels welcome and has equal opportunities. Yet, responsibility does not lie solely with institutions: civil society must also play an active role in promoting tolerance and intercultural dialogue. Although Portugal is often praised for its hospitality, there is still a long way to go. Neighborhoods like Martim Moniz in Lisbon or the Mouraria area are vibrant examples of multiculturalism, where different cultures coexist and influence each other. However, not all immigrants have access to these spaces of coexistence, as many live in precarious conditions or peripheral areas. For Portugal to truly become a multicultural and inclusive country, a joint effort is necessary. As long as public policies are more assertive and society more empathetic, we can build a nation where everyone, regardless of their origin, feels like an integral part. After all, a country’s wealth is not measured only by its GDP but also by its ability to welcome and integrate those who seek a new life within its borders.
